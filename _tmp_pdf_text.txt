We present an eﬃcient kinetic Monte Carlo scheme to determine the Helmholtz free energy and entropy of bulk
ﬂuids and adsorption systems. The method is made possible because this technique enables the accurate de-
termination of the chemical potential. The Helmholtz free energy, A, is obtained by integrating the chemical
potential with respect to the number of molecules, at constant volume and temperature, and the entropy is then
determined from the fundamental thermodynamic equation A = E − TS. The entropy of bulk argon is found to
be in excellent agreement with values calculated from the established equation of state (EOS). In a system with
two co-existing phases we show that our method can determine the surface tension at the Gibbs dividing surface,
without recourse to the mechanical route of Irving, Kirkwood and Buﬀ.

We show that the intrinsic integral molecular Helmholtz free energy and entropy of the adsorbed phase,
corrected for the surface excess, are independent of the size of the simulation box for all chemical potentials
tested.

The new procedure is illustrated for a range of gases commonly used in the characterization of porous solids
(argon, nitrogen, carbon dioxide and ammonia) as adsorbates and a graphitic slit pore as the model adsorbent.

1. Introduction

The Metropolis Monte Carlo (mMC) simulation method, based on
importance sampling, was ingeniously developed by scientists at the
Los Alamos National Laboratory in the US, and has been widely used by
scientists and engineers to determine equilibrium properties in nu-
merous problems in chemistry and chemical engineering [1]. More
recently, an alternative scheme based on the kinetic Monte Carlo (kMC)
has been developed, and applied to a number of problems in colloid
chemistry and to adsorption on surfaces and in conﬁned spaces [2–8].
For example, various adsorbate transitions have been explored sys-
tematically where it was found that kMC oﬀers distinct advantages over
mMC [3] in systems with very high densities [9]. To ensure that equi-
librium has been established, it is necessary to check the equality of
chemical potential at all points in the system [9,10]. In traditional
mMC, the chemical potential is usually determined by the Widom
method [11,12], in which ghost particles are inserted into a number of
frozen conﬁgurations. The accuracy of this method depends critically
on the number of insertions, and decreases with increasing density, and
is therefore very computationally intensive. By contrast, kMC provides
an eﬃcient route for calculating the chemical potential at all densities
[10,13].

Other thermodynamic properties (i.e. Helmholtz free energy and
entropy), are rarely studied in simulations, since there is no simple and
satisfactory method for their determination in mMC [1]. The various
methods that have been proposed [14–17], are either elaborate or
cumbersome to apply [1]. Possibly the most beneﬁcial feature of kMC is
the accurate determination of the chemical potential [10] and we can
take advantage of this to determine the Helmholtz free energy and the
entropy. Moreover, in non-homogenous systems (e.g. phase co-existence
and adsorption systems), the Helmholtz free energy and entropy can be
decomposed into contributions from the constituent phases of the
system, allowing an analysis of the speciﬁc contributions to free energy
and the entropy from these phases.

Surface tension, associated with the boundary between two co-ex-
isting phases, is commonly determined by the mechanical route of
Irving and Kirkwood [18] or Kirkwood and Buﬀ [19]. Goujon et al. for
example [20], employed this route to obtain the surface tension, and
investigated the eﬀects on the calculated energy of the cut-oﬀ radius,
the long range correction, the three-body interaction, the box size and
shifting the potential to zero at the cut-oﬀ radius.

In this work we use accurate values of chemical potential, de-
termined by kMC, to ﬁnd the Helmholtz free energy and entropy, and as
an alternative to the mechanical method, to calculate the surface

2. Theory

2.1. Kinetic Monte Carlo

A feature that distinguishes kMC from mMC is the absence of any
acceptance criterion in a trial move. In mMC, many trial moves are
rejected when the system energy is highly positive, due to repulsive
overlap. In kMC all moves are accepted, even when there is a large
overlap between molecules. Conﬁgurations with a strongly overlapping
pair have an extremely short lifetime because one of them is highly
likely to be selected in the move to generate the next conﬁguration. The
Rosenbluth scheme [1] governing this procedure is outlined brieﬂy in
the following sections.

3. Results

3.1. Bulk ﬂuid

We show in Fig. 3 the four basic thermodynamic variables: pressure,
chemical potential, molar Helmholtz free energy (via the thermo-
dynamic route) and molar entropy of the bulk ﬂuid argon at sub-critical
temperatures of 87 K, 120 K and a supercritical temperature of 240 K.
The simulated results from our method (with long range correction
added to the molecular energy) are in excellent agreement with the EOS
of Johnson et al. [30] and with the results obtained earlier by Fan et al.
[10]. The percentage error of the chemical potential obtained with kMC
scheme is typically less than 0.1%, showing the accuracy of the che-
mical potential. The results for other ﬂuids are presented in Appendix B
to demonstrate that the method is applicable equally to polyatomic and
polar ﬂuids.

In Fig. 4, we compare the Helmholtz free energy determined by the

virial route with the thermodynamic route, and note that there is ex-
cellent agreement. However, the success of the virial route depends
critically on the determination of the pressure, which may not be ac-
curate for dense systems. This is illustrated in Fig. 4a where the
Helmholtz free energy of the dense phase at 87 K is fairly noisy, but if
the pressure is determined with a suﬃciently long simulation, it is able
to produce a more accurate result than the thermodynamic route, since
this avoids the accumulation of errors in the process of integration
(Fig. 4b). In addition, the virial route is strictly valid only for uniform
systems and the thermodynamic route is the only method available for
non-uniform system. An accurate determination of bulk ﬂuid properties
is important in our assessment of the thermodynamic properties of the
adsorption systems as we demonstrate below.

3.1.1. Eﬀect of simulation box size

The pressure and the chemical potential shown in Fig. 3 exhibit a
typical van der Waals (vdW) loop for temperatures (87 K and 120 K)
below the bulk critical temperature. In Fig. 5 we explore the eﬀects of
the box size on the vdW behaviour for the four thermodynamic vari-
ables: pressure, chemical potential, molar Helmholtz free energy and
molar entropy. The box size clearly aﬀects the evolution of the vdW
loop, notably for the chemical potential: for small box sizes, the vdW
loop has a clear S-shape because of the small ﬂuctuations and because
the number of molecules in the system is not suﬃcient to form stable
co-existing phases. The regions that show an increase in the chemical
potential with respect to the number of molecules are either stable or
metastable, while the region that shows a decrease is unstable. This S-
shape is similar to that observed in the classical work of Hansen and
Verlet [31] who suppressed ﬂuctuations by dividing the box into re-
gions and constraining the variation in the number of molecules in
those regions. As the box size is increased, the vdW loop changes from a
sigmoid shape to a step because the system forms two stable phases as
evidenced by the constant chemical potential within the vdW loop. The
evolution of the system into two stable co-existing phases has been
studied by a number of workers [32,33]. Interestingly, the molar en-
tropy, is not aﬀected by the system size in the stable regions of gas and
liquid phases. The entropy decreases with the number of molecules as
expected because of the reduction in the degree of freedom as the

3.2. Adsorption

We take adsorption of argon on a graphite surface as an example of
the application of our method to determine the Helmholtz free energy
of non-uniform adsorption systems. Fig. 6 shows plots of the excess

density versus the chemical potential for three diﬀerent box sizes. These
isotherms very nearly overlap, indicating that the excess amount is
independent of the box size.

The integral molar Helmholtz free energies for the above three box
sizes are shown in Fig. 7. With the exception of the sub-monolayer
region, the molar Helmholtz free energy is smaller in larger boxes be-
cause there is a larger contribution from the molecules in the gas phase
in the larger boxes. Therefore, it is informative to determine the free
energy intrinsic to the adsorbed phase, by calculating the excess
Helmholtz free energy using Eq. (19). The inset in Fig. 7 shows that the
excess Helmholtz free energy is the same for all three box sizes.

Fig. 8a shows that the same conclusion can be reached for the molar
entropy. In the sub-monolayer region, the integral molar entropy de-
creases to a minimum when the ﬁrst adsorbate layer is complete be-
cause there is a decrease in the number of available conﬁgurations. The
approach to the entropy minimum is diﬀerent for diﬀerent box sizes;
again because there is a larger contribution from molecules in the gas
phase in the larger boxes but, as found for the Helmholtz energy, the
excess molar entropy is the same for all box sizes as shown in Fig. 8b.

The minimum molar entropy at the monolayer density is 53 J/mol/K,
which is slightly lower than the molar entropy of the pure liquid
(54.7 J/mol/K), suggesting that the adsorbed phase behaves like a
compressed liquid at completion of the ﬁrst layer. Beyond the mono-
layer region, the molar entropy increases and becomes asymptotic to
the molar entropy of the pure liquid, indicating that the multi-layered
adsorbed ﬁlm resembles the bulk liquid.

Nitrogen adsorbed on graphite at 77 K has a ﬁrst order transition
from a supercritical state to a commensurate solid in the ﬁrst layer,
which is also exhibited for adsorption in a slit shaped mesopore, prior to
a ﬁrst order capillary condensation. A method for the determination of
the Helmholtz free energy, when the chemical potential plot vs the
number of molecules shows a ﬁrst order transition, is given in Appendix
D. The isotherm for nitrogen adsorption on a graphite surface is pre-
sented in Fig. 9a, and shows a ﬁrst order transition when the surface
density reaches 9.3 µmol/m2. At this density, the excess molar entropy
is 43 J/mol/K, compared to the molar entropy of the bulk liquid ni-
trogen of 47 J/mol/K, indicating that the ﬁrst layer is in a supercritical
state prior to the transition. The ﬁrst order transition changes the state
of the adsorbate from a supercritical state to a commensurate state, as
evidenced by the density of 10.54 µmol/m2, theoretically calculated for
a commensurate packing on graphite [35]. This transition is associated
with a sharp decrease in the entropy, to 31 J/mol/K, a value that is
much lower than that of the bulk liquid. In fact, a sharp decrease in the
entropy occurs whenever there is a ﬁrst order transition in the ad-
sorbate. To illustrate this point, we present the adsorption isotherm for
argon in a 2 nm open-ended graphitic slit pore in Fig. 9b, which shows
that a capillary condensation (ﬁrst order transition) occurs at 8.5 kPa.
This transition has been discussed in a previous paper [30]. The

interesting feature here is that the molar excess entropy decreases
sharply to 42 J/mol/K, which is well below the molar entropy of the
bulk liquid. This indicates that the argon is strongly adsorbed in a 2 nm
pore and forms a highly compressed liquid which is corroborated by the
fact that the pore density is much higher than the bulk liquid density
(35 kmol/m3).

4. Conclusion

In this paper we have presented an eﬃcient scheme for the accurate
determination of the chemical potential, the molar Helmholtz free en-
ergy and the molar entropy using a kinetic Monte Carlo method. This
scheme is discussed in detail for argon and further illustrated in the
Appendix for a range of molecules. The molar Helmholtz free energy
and the molar entropy for the bulk ﬂuids agree well with results from
the relevant equations of state. In addition, using the excess Helmholtz
free energy at the Gibbs interface, we are able to determine the surface
tension when two phases co-exist, oﬀering an alternative to the me-
chanical route. The concept of excess used to determine the amount
adsorbed in adsorption systems is applied to determine the excess
(adsorbed) molecular Helmholtz free energy and entropy, which are
found to be independent of the size of the simulation box for a given
pressure. Finally, we explored the application of the excess entropy to
adsorption systems in a mesopore where there is a phase transition.
